# Instagram caption

In 1966, a nurse in Queens drew up a front door that could show her who was outside.

Marie Van Brittan Brown often worked irregular hours. She wanted to see, and speak to, whoever was at her door without opening it. With her husband, electronics technician Albert L. Brown, she designed a system that did exactly that:

📷 A camera that slid up and down behind four peepholes, so it could line up with visitors of different heights
📺 Video sent by radio to a screen in another room
🎙️ Two-way audio between the door and the screen
🔓 A button that released a radio-controlled lock
🚨 An alarm that could alert a guard, police officer or watchman

They filed for a patent on August 1, 1966. U.S. Patent 3,482,037, with Marie listed first and Albert as co-inventor, was granted on December 2, 1969.

Their complete system was never widely manufactured during her lifetime. Today, her design is widely recognized as a predecessor of the video doorbells and home cameras many of us check without a second thought.

# Facebook caption

Before video doorbells, there was Marie Van Brittan Brown.

In the 1960s, Marie was a nurse living in Jamaica, Queens, often working irregular hours. She wanted a safer way to know who was at her door without opening it. Together with her husband, Albert L. Brown, an electronics technician, she designed a home-security system built around television surveillance.

How it worked, according to their patent:

• Four peepholes ran down the front door, and a motor-driven camera slid up and down behind them to get the best view of the visitor.
• The picture was sent by radio to a screen in another room, such as the bedroom.
• Microphones and speakers let the person inside talk with the visitor.
• A button could release a radio-controlled lock to let someone in.
• An alarm button could alert a guard, police officer or watchman at a security station. The patent even describes a recorder for the conversation.

The Browns filed their application on August 1, 1966. U.S. Patent 3,482,037, "Home Security System Utilizing Television Surveillance," was granted on December 2, 1969. Marie is listed first, with Albert as co-inventor.

She didn't invent cameras, intercoms or alarms. Her contribution was bringing them together into one system for the home, decades before that idea became an everyday product. The complete system was never widely manufactured in her lifetime, and she died in Queens in 1999. Today her design is widely recognized as a predecessor of modern video doorbells and residential security systems.
